## 1. miR-9 and miR-124: gene structure and brain development

### 1.1 Loci and strands

miR-9 and miR-124 are each encoded by three independent genomic loci in mammals (*miR-9-1/2/3*, *miR-124-1/2/3*) producing identical mature sequences, whereas several other model organisms carry a single gene per family ([Yoo & Crabtree 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2958854)). The *miR-9* hairpin yields two functionally distinct strands: miR-9-5p (conventionally "miR-9") and miR-9-3p ("miR-9\*"), with separate seed sequences and separate targets ([Coolen et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3834235/)).

### 1.2 miR-9 targets in neurogenesis

### 1.4 The triple-negative circuit and reprogramming

The npBAF → nBAF transition is mediated by repression of BAF53a by miR-9\* and miR-124 through recognition sites in the BAF53a 3′ UTR; expressing REST in post-mitotic neurons de-represses BAF53a, placing REST upstream of the switch ([Yoo et al. 2009](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08139)). Expression of miR-9/9\* and miR-124 together converts human fibroblasts into neurons, a process facilitated by NEUROD2 ([Yoo et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10323)). Acting jointly — but neither alone — the two miRNAs suppress Rap2a, promoting NSC differentiation and dendritic branching ([Xu et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4879704)).

## 2. miR-9 and miR-124 in the male germ line

### 2.1 miR-9

In the *Drosophila* testis, miR-9a is expressed in germline stem cells (not in spermatocytes or the somatic hub/cyst cells) and rises with age. It directly targets *N-cadherin*, limiting GSC–hub adhesion so that stem cells detach and differentiate; *miR-9a*-null males retain more stem cells but have reduced fertility, and N-cad overexpression phenocopies this ([Epstein et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5605507)).

### 2.2 miR-124

- ***Drosophila*:** miR-124 mutant males show increased male–male courtship and reduced reproductive success. The sex-specific splicing factor *transformer* is the functionally significant target, with miR-124 limiting inappropriate expression of the female form ([Weng & Cohen 2013](https://elifesciences.org/articles/640)).
- **Mouse spermatids:** miR-124 targets **flotillin-2 (FLOT2)**, a Golgi-associated protein in acrosome biogenesis. Luciferase assays confirmed direct targeting; intratesticular miR-124 mimic caused abnormal acrosome morphology and downregulated FLOT2 and caveolin-1, implicating caveolin-independent vesicle trafficking ([Wu et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551675)).

## 3. The machinery of intercellular bridge trafficking

### 3.1 The bridges themselves

TEX14 is required to convert midbodies into stable germ-cell intercellular bridges. *Tex14*-null males lack bridges entirely by electron microscopy, arrest spermatogenesis, and are sterile ([Greenbaum et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0505123103)). TEX14 colocalises with the centralspindlin components MKLP1 (KIF23) and MgcRacGAP and converts these midbody matrix proteins into stable bridge components ([Greenbaum et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717030)).

### 3.2 What crosses the bridges

Direct time-lapse imaging of rat early spermatids showed small (\~0.5 µm) granules moving continuously across bridges, of which 28% actually completed transit. The Golgi-processed haploid gene product TRA54 was transported in granules into the acrosome system of the neighbouring spermatid. The \~1.8 µm **chromatoid body** itself was observed crossing a bridge. Microtubule inhibitors abolished all organelle movement through bridges and disintegrated the chromatoid body ([Ventelä et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC165675)).

### 3.3 The chromatoid body as miRNA hub

Dicer and microRNP components — Ago proteins and microRNAs — are highly concentrated in chromatoid bodies, which function as intracellular centres of the miRNA pathway; Dicer interacts there with the germ-cell helicase MVH ([Kotaja et al. 2006a](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0509333103)).

### 3.4 KIF17b: the three-way hinge

| **Timing** | KIF17b-mediated relocalisation of ACT to the cytoplasm temporally correlates with cessation of transcription of CREM-regulated genes | [Kotaja et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC489983) |

| **mRNA transport, incl. between cells** | KIF17b coimmunoprecipitates with TB-RBP in an RNP carrying specific CREM-regulated mRNAs; KIF17b and TB-RBP dissociate sequentially, with TB-RBP release coinciding with translation. ### 3.5 CREM

CREM-mutant mice arrest at the first step of spermiogenesis, lack late spermatids entirely, show significantly increased germ-cell apoptosis, and produce no spermatozoa ([Nantel et al. 1996](https://www.nature.com/articles/380159a0)). *Crem* loss deregulates over 4,700 testis genes, including 101 spermatogenesis-associated genes, 41 of which are directly CREM-bound by ChIP-seq ([Košir et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3285179)). CREM's postmeiotic activity is phosphorylation-independent and depends on ACT, which is expressed specifically in haploid round and elongating spermatids ([Kotaja et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC489983)).

### 3.6 Why bridge sharing matters evolutionarily

Sharing of gene products across cytoplasmic bridges is the mechanism that normally equalises phenotype between haploid spermatids. Single-cell RNA-seq identified a large class of **genoinformative markers (GIMs)** — genes whose allelic expression ratio tracks the haploid genotype because they are *not* fully shared across bridges — a subset of which behave as selfish elements driving transmission ratio distortion in mouse, cattle, and human ([Bhutani et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb1723)).

## 4. OGT, OGA, and MAPT

### 4.1 OGT and OGA: what is established

| Fact | Source |
| --- | --- |

| Conditional *Ogt* loss in neurons causes tau hyperphosphorylation; O-GlcNAc modulates phosphorylation across conserved signalling pathways | [O'Donnell et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14749383/) |
| **O-GlcNAc in testis is localised to differentiating spermatogonia and spermatocytes, and is low or apparently absent in round spermatids, elongated spermatids, Sertoli cells and interstitial cells** | [Qian et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10185674/) |
| Blocking OGA pharmacologically (Thiamet-G) in young mice raises O-GlcNAc and recapitulates the aged phenotype: pachytene arrest from synapsis and recombination defects. An OGT inhibitor partially rescues aged testes | [Qian et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10185674/) |
| In *C. elegans*, *ogt-1* deletion reduces male brood size by 65–80% and transfers fewer sperm; two *oga-1* alleles do **not** affect male fertility. Rescue is achieved by hypodermal expression, indicating a non-catalytic, non-germline mechanism | [Konzman et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9710795) |

### 4.2 OGT as a direct regulator of motor-driven transport

This is the clearest mechanistic link between OGT and trafficking, though it is established in neurons, not spermatids:

> OGT O-GlcNAcylates the mitochondrial motor-adaptor **Milton/TRAK**, and this modification arrests mitochondrial motility. The GlcNAcylation state of Milton tracks extracellular glucose, and OGT alters mitochondrial motility *in vivo* ([Pekkurnaz et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4224014)).

OGT also acts on the track itself: O-GlcNAcylated peptides map to α-tubulin residues 173–185 and β-tubulin 216–238; increased α-tubulin O-GlcNAcylation reduces heterodimerisation, and O-GlcNAcylated tubulin does not polymerise, so the modification negatively regulates microtubule formation ([Ji et al. 2011](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A192593)). In ciliated cells, both α-tubulin and HDAC6 are O-GlcNAcylated *in vivo*, and O-GlcNAcylation of either promotes microtubule disassembly and ciliary shortening ([Tian & Qin 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2019.01.031)).

### 4.3 Why OGT/OGA are probably *not* direct regulators of spermatid bridge traffic

No published study places OGT, OGA, or O-GlcNAc at germ-cell intercellular bridges, nor tests bridge transit in *Ogt* or *Oga* germ-cell mutants. The strongest available negative evidence is expression-based: O-GlcNAc is essentially absent from round and elongated spermatids, which is precisely the window in which bridge-mediated organelle traffic occurs ([Qian et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10185674/) vs. [Ventelä et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC165675)). The *C. elegans* data point the same way — the fertility defect was rescued by hypodermal, not germline, expression ([Konzman et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9710795)). On present evidence the O-GlcNAc cycle acts upstream of spermiogenesis, in meiotic entry and progression, rather than within the haploid syncytium.

### 4.4 A note on OTOG/OTOGL

If "OTG" was intended as *OTOG*/*OTOGL*: although *OTOGL* carries a high mutant ratio (13.99%) in a screen of 336 non-obstructive azoospermia patients and is expressed in postnatal mouse testis, global *Otogl* knockout mice develop normally, are fertile, and show all spermatogenic cell types in the seminiferous tubules, so OTOGL is nonessential for spermatogenesis ([Li et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12958-021-00779-0)). There is no reported connection to intercellular bridges.

### 4.5 MAPT (tau)

**Expression in the germ line is established.** Tau was identified biochemically, molecularly, and immunologically in rat and bovine testis; in bull testis it localises to the **spermatid manchette**, the transient cross-linked microtubule array, and tau immunoreactivity appears in late stage I spermatids before manchette formation, suggesting a role in manchette assembly ([Ashman et al. 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1547309)). In human tissue, tau is located in the sperm midpiece and shows immunoreactivity in both spermatocytes and spermatids ([Mottier et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4236348)).

**Tau's transport function is established — in neurons.** Single-molecule assays on tau-decorated microtubules showed that **dynein tends to reverse direction whereas kinesin tends to detach** at tau patches; kinesin is inhibited at about a tenth of the tau concentration that inhibits dynein, and the microtubule-binding domain alone suffices. This differential modulation means MAPs can spatially bias the balance of plus- vs. minus-end-directed transport ([Dixit et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2866193)).

**Tau is the point where OGT/OGA and the transport machinery converge.** Tau O-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation are reciprocal: the OGA inhibitor Thiamet-G (Ki = 21 nM) raised O-GlcNAc and reduced tau phosphorylation at Thr231, Ser396 and Ser422 in rat cortex and hippocampus ([Yuzwa et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18587388/)). At the residue level, Ser400 O-GlcNAcylation reduces Ser404 phosphorylation by CDK2/cyclinA3 and interrupts GSK3β-mediated sequential phosphorylation; reciprocally, phosphorylation of S396/S404 suppresses S400 O-GlcNAcylation ([Smet-Nocca et al. 2011](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2011/mb/c0mb00337a)).

**Not established:** no study has tested whether tau is present at, or functions in, germ-cell intercellular bridges; whether tau's kinesin-detaching/dynein-reversing behaviour operates on manchette or bridge-adjacent microtubules; or whether tau O-GlcNAcylation status changes across spermiogenesis.

## 5. The tubulin code: glycylation, glycylation enzymes, and motor selection

### 5.1 Enzymes and genes

In mammals, glycylation proceeds in two steps: **TTLL3** and **TTLL8** are the initiating glycylases adding the first glycines to unmodified tubulin, while **TTLL10** exclusively elongates nascent glycine chains ([Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590)). The **human TTLL10 orthologue has lost enzymatic activity through two amino acid changes**, implying that monoglycylation suffices for glycylation's functions in humans ([Rogowski et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2009.05.020)).

Glycylation is restricted to the axonemal tubulin of the outer doublets and is absent from central-pair microtubules; TTLL3's own flagellar localisation is IFT-dependent ([Kubo et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11244163/)).

### 5.2 What glycylation does: axoneme stabilisation and length control

Glycylation is the one tubulin PTM that is essentially confined to cilia and flagella ([Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590)), and its best-documented function is **structural stabilisation** rather than track-marking. The evidence comes from four independent tissues, each with a different glycylase complement, which is what makes the stabilisation role convincing:

**Motile cilia — outright disassembly without it.** Codepletion of TTLL3 and TTLL8 leads to disassembly of motile cilia in ependymal cells, establishing glycylation as essential for axonemal stability ([Bosch Grau et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1083/jcb.201305041)).

**Photoreceptor connecting cilia — shortening and degeneration.** Only one glycylase, TTLL3, is expressed in retina. *Ttll3−/−* mice lack photoreceptor glycylation, which shortens the connecting cilia and causes slow retinal degeneration ([Bosch Grau et al. 2017](https://integ04-cnspfv.archimed.fr/doc/OAI_1/oai-HAL-hal-02376288v1)).

**Primary cilia — length is set by glycylation level.** Glycylation accumulates in primary cilia in a length-dependent manner; depleting or overexpressing glycylases modulates primary cilium length, and cilia-targeted TTLL3 overexpression lengthens them ([Gadadhar et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584158)). In colon, where TTLL3 is the sole glycylase, *Ttll3* knockout reduces primary cilium number, raises the division rate of colon epithelium, and strongly promotes tumour development in a tumorigenesis model; reduced TTLL3 expression tracks with human colorectal carcinoma ([Rocha et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.15252/embj.201488466)).

**Sperm flagella — built correctly, but unstable in function.** Importantly, glycylation is *not* required for assembly. *Ttll3−/− Ttll8−/−* sperm flagella are correctly built and present in all genotypes, with sperm counts and viability nearly identical to controls; what fails is coordinated beating ([Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590)). So in flagella glycylation is a *regulator of motor behaviour*, whereas in ependymal and connecting cilia its loss is *structurally destabilising*. Both readings are supported; they are not in conflict, because the axonemes differ in turnover and mechanical load.

### 5.3 Mono- vs. polyglycylation, and competition with glutamylation

The initiation/elongation split is functionally meaningful. TTLL3 and TTLL8 add the first glycines; TTLL10 only elongates them into polyglycine chains ([Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590)). Because **human TTLL10 has lost catalytic activity through two amino acid changes**, the authors proposed that monoglycylation suffices for glycylation's functions — i.e. humans are a natural monoglycylation-only system ([Rogowski et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2009.05.020)). Mammalian single-knockouts (*Ttll3−/−* or *Ttll8−/−*) only partially abolish sperm glycylation; the double knockout abolishes it entirely ([Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590)).

Glycylation and polyglutamylation **compete for the same modification sites** on the tubulin C-terminal tails. Loss of glycylation in *Ttll3−/−* photoreceptors raises tubulin glutamylation; conversely, the hyperglutamylation of the *pcd* (Purkinje cell degeneration) mouse abolishes glycylation. Unbalancing the equilibrium in either direction causes retinal degeneration ([Bosch Grau et al. 2017](https://integ04-cnspfv.archimed.fr/doc/OAI_1/oai-HAL-hal-02376288v1)). This is the mechanistic reason glycylation cannot be treated as an independent variable: changing it necessarily changes the glutamylation state that governs kinesin-1, KIF1A and dynein-2 behaviour (see §5.5).

Enzyme recruitment is also hierarchical and cross-regulated: TTLL10 binding requires prior monoglycylation by TTLL8, is enhanced by TTLL6-mediated polyglutamylation, and is progressively inhibited as polyglycylation accumulates ([Chen et al., *eLife* reviewed preprint](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/98040v1)) — **preprint; provisional.**

## 6. KIF17b and ACT: is the shuttling model correct, and does KIF17b exist in humans?

### 6.1 Is the claim true?

**Yes, as stated — with one important correction about mechanism.** The original finding: KIF17b was pulled out of a yeast two-hybrid screen using full-length ACT as bait; it is highly and exclusively expressed in testis; it co-expresses with ACT during testis development; the two co-immunoprecipitate from mouse testis extract; and in transfected COS and NIH3T3 cells they fully colocalise. ACT is nuclear but becomes cytoplasmic at a specific point in spermatid maturation, coincident with its depletion from the nucleus. Excess KIF17b inhibits transcription from two CREM-dependent promoter–reporter constructs, and that activation is recovered by sequestering the ACT–KIF17b complex in the nucleus with leptomycin B — implicating CRM1-dependent nuclear export ([Macho et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077265)).

In haploid spermatids *in vivo*, KIF17b colocalises with ACT and mediates its nucleus→cytoplasm transport during spermatid elongation, and this relocalisation temporally correlates with the cessation of transcription of CREM-regulated genes ([Kotaja et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC489983)).

**The correction:** this is *not* conventional kinesin cargo-hauling. KIF17b's ability to shuttle between nucleus and cytoplasm and to transport ACT depends on **neither its motor domain nor on microtubules** — a microtubule-independent function for a kinesin. PKA phosphorylates KIF17b, and this modification governs its subcellular localisation, so cAMP signalling controls CREM-mediated transcription through KIF17b ([Kotaja et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M505971200)). So the accurate phrasing is: KIF17b binds ACT and shuttles between compartments to regulate transcription, but it does so as a cAMP-regulated nucleocytoplasmic carrier, not as a microtubule motor. Its *separate*, motor-dependent job is transporting CREM-regulated mRNAs with TB-RBP ([Chennathukuzhi et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC307608)).

A caveat worth carrying: the strongest colocalisation and reporter data come from heterologous transfected somatic cells; the *in vivo* spermatid evidence is correlative (colocalisation plus temporal correlation with transcriptional shutdown), not a germ-cell *Kif17b* conditional knockout. I did not find a germ-cell-specific *Kif17* knockout testing the ACT-export model directly \[verify — worth a targeted search if this matters for your purposes\].

### 6.2 Does KIF17b exist in humans and primates?

**The gene does; the testis-specific isoform question is less cleanly settled in the peer-reviewed literature than the mouse work is.**

What is directly supported:

- **A human testis KIF17b transcript was cloned.** Chennathukuzhi et al. amplified a partial human EST clone (GenBank AF009624, 937 bp, encoding the kinesin C-terminus) by RT-PCR **from human testis RNA**; they then used the incompletely spliced human brain clone KIAA1405 to amplify a 2 kb partial cDNA, again from human testis RNA, and completed the 5′ end by RLM-RACE from human testis RNA, confirming the sequence against the human genomic locus ([Chennathukuzhi et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC307608)). That is direct evidence of a human testis-expressed KIF17 transcript obtained by the same group that characterised mouse KIF17b.
- **The human partner protein is present.** ACT/FHL5 was cloned and its expression demonstrated in human testicular tissue ([Palermo et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11327716)). *Fhl5*/*Act* expression is reported not only in rodents but also in human ([Lindsey et al. 2009](https://rbej.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1477-7827-7-133)).
- **Reviews from the originating group assert conservation.** The CREM–ACT–KIF17b circuit and its regulatory functions are described as conserved in human spermatogenesis, with the explicit expectation that this bears on human infertility ([Krausz & Sassone-Corsi 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15705296)). This is a review assertion, not a primary demonstration.

What is *not* established from the papers I verified:

- No peer-reviewed paper I found demonstrates a **human or primate testis-specific KIF17 splice isoform structurally equivalent to mouse KIF17b**, nor tests the ACT-export function in human or non-human primate germ cells. The human cloning in [Chennathukuzhi et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC307608) establishes a testis transcript; it does not by itself establish that the isoform structure or the testis-exclusivity matches mouse. \[verify\]
- Mouse KIF17b was introduced as "an isoform of the brain-specific kinesin KIF17" that is highly and exclusively testis-expressed ([Macho et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077265)); whether human KIF17 shows the same reciprocal brain/testis isoform partition has not, as far as I found, been directly reported.

For reference only (database, not peer-reviewed): Ensembl lists **KIF17B** as a synonym of human *KIF17* (ENSG00000117245, chromosome 1p36.12), with 11 annotated transcripts and 193 orthologues — consistent with the gene being broadly conserved and alternatively spliced, but not evidence about the specific isoform's function.

**Relevance to primate comparative work:** since *FHL5* and *KIF17* are both present with annotated primate orthologues, the circuit is testable comparatively — but the current literature supports "the genes are there and a human testis transcript exists" rather than "the mouse KIF17b isoform and its ACT-shuttling function are demonstrated in primates."

## 7. Synthesis: a defensible causal chain, and where it breaks

**Established, end to end:**

1. Bridges exist and are essential (TEX14) — [Greenbaum et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0505123103).
2. Granules, Golgi-derived cargo, and the whole chromatoid body physically transit bridges, and this transit is microtubule-dependent — [Ventelä et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC165675).
3. The chromatoid body concentrates Dicer, Ago and miRNAs — [Kotaja et al. 2006a](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0509333103).
4. KIF17b is both the CB's associated motor (via MIWI) and the motor for intercellular CREM-mRNA transport with TB-RBP — [Kotaja et al. 2006b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16787948); [Chennathukuzhi et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC307608).
5. KIF17b simultaneously sets the *timing* of the CREM transcriptional programme by exporting ACT — [Macho et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077265); [Kotaja et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC489983).
6. Microtubule PTMs select which motors run and how well — [Sirajuddin et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117587); [McKenney et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888239); [Gadadhar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612590).
7. Incomplete sharing across bridges is the substrate for sperm-level selection — [Bhutani et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb1723).

**Where the chain breaks:**

- No study places **miR-9 or miR-124 specifically** inside the chromatoid body; [Kotaja et al. 2006a](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0509333103) established this for microRNAs as a class.
- miR-124's targeting of **PTBP1** is established in neurons ([Makeyev et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2007.07.015)) but has not been tested in spermatids, despite the same PTBP1→PTBP2 switch being essential there.
- **OGT/OGA** have no reported presence or function at intercellular bridges, and O-GlcNAc is largely absent from the spermatid stages where bridge traffic happens ([Qian et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10185674/)).
- **MAPT** is present in spermatids and the manchette ([Ashman et al. 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1547309)) and has a well-characterised motor-sorting function in neurons ([Dixit et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2866193)), but the two have never been connected experimentally in germ cells.
- Whether **TTLL10's** loss of catalytic activity in humans ([Rogowski et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2009.05.020)) has consequences for bridge-adjacent or manchette microtubules — as distinct from the axoneme — is untested.
