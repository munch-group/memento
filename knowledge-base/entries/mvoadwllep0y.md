# Population genetic diversity in Africa

Oct 23, 2026 · @Kasper

Africa holds the deepest and most structured human genetic variation of any continent, and the smallest share of the world's sequenced genomes.

## How much variation Africa carries

African genomes hold more variation than those of any other continent, and the catalogue is still being extended rather than completed. Whole-genome sequencing of 426 individuals from 50 ethnolinguistic groups in 13 countries recovered more than 3 million previously undescribed variants, most of them in groups sampled for the first time ([Choudhury et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2859-7)).

The excess is not only in single-nucleotide variants. Reads from 910 people of African descent that failed to align to GRCh38 assembled into 296,485,284 bp across 125,715 contigs — roughly 10% more DNA than the reference genome contains ([Sherman et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-018-0273-y)).

The population-genetic signature is consistent across statistics: African populations show the highest genetic diversity, the lowest levels of linkage disequilibrium, the largest long-term effective population sizes, and the deepest split times of all human lineages ([Pfennig et al. 2023](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/15/4/evad054/7092825)). Within the continent the gradient peaks among hunter-gatherer groups: mean heterozygosity in the Khoe-San is reported at 1.154 × 10⁻³ against 1.09 × 10⁻³ in the Mandenka of West Africa ([Pfennig et al. 2023](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/15/4/evad054/7092825)).

For mapping, low LD is the operative property: haplotype blocks are short, so association signals localise more finely than in Eurasian samples, but tag-SNP panels and imputation reference sets built on Eurasian LD transfer poorly.

## Deep structure and the archaic question

The deepest divergence among living humans is African, and recent work has reframed it as a network rather than a clean split. Modelling of linkage-disequilibrium and diversity statistics across eastern, western and southern African genomes — including 44 newly sequenced Nama (Khoe-San) individuals — places the earliest divergence among contemporary populations at 120,000 to 135,000 years ago, preceded by two or more weakly differentiated ancestral populations connected by gene flow over hundreds of thousands of years ([Ragsdale et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06055-y)).

That figure sits against an older estimate of 350,000 to 260,000 years for the first modern human population divergence, derived from seven ancient genomes from KwaZulu-Natal ([Schlebusch et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao6266)). The gap is mostly a difference between clean-split and structured-stem model assumptions rather than a disagreement about the data.

Ancient DNA from Shum Laka in Cameroon adds a second axis. Four children buried roughly 8,000 and 3,000 years ago are genetically closest to present-day hunter-gatherers of western Central Africa, which implies that people in western Cameroon today — and Bantu speakers generally — are not substantially descended from that population; the inferred Africa-wide phylogeny shows widespread admixture and three radiations, one producing at least four deep lineages ([Lipson et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1929-1)).

The archaic question is open. Site-frequency-spectrum analysis of four West African populations assigns 2 to 19% of their ancestry to an archaic population that diverged before the Neanderthal–modern human split ([Durvasula & Sankararaman 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax5097)). Weakly structured stem models reproduce the same polymorphism patterns without any archaic contribution, and attribute only 1 to 4% of genetic differentiation among contemporary populations to drift between stem populations ([Ragsdale et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06055-y)). Both remain viable; separating them would need archaic African genomes, which do not exist.

The deep lineages sit at opposite ends of the continent: Khoe-San and Nama in the south-west, Central African hunter-gatherers in the Congo basin, with the two ancient-DNA anchors — Shum Laka in Cameroon and Mota Cave in Ethiopia — on either side of the forest belt.

## The Bantu expansion

The expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples is the dominant demographic event of Late Holocene Africa, and the genetic evidence now supports a serial-founder model. Genotype data for 1,763 individuals from 147 populations across 14 African countries, together with 12 Late Iron Age genomes, show genetic diversity among Bantu-speaking populations declining with distance from western Africa, consistent with repeated founder events along the expansion front ([Fortes-Lima et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06770-6)). The same study places the start of the expansion 6,000 to 4,000 years ago in western Africa and identifies present-day Zambia and the Democratic Republic of Congo as crossroads where separate routes interacted. Independent whole-genome data from H3Africa cohorts likewise point to Zambia as an intermediate site along the expansion routes ([Choudhury et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2859-7)).

Bantu-speaking populations today number about 350 million people speaking more than 500 languages over roughly 9 million km² ([Fortes-Lima et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06770-6)).

The route is still contested. Genomic data from 1,318 individuals in 35 western Central African populations indicate that early Bantu speakers first moved south through the equatorial rainforest before turning east and south ([Patin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal1988)). A dated phylogeny of roughly 400 Bantu languages instead has migrants following emerging savannah corridors, with rainforest habitat repeatedly acting as a temporal barrier and delaying occupation by about 300 years on average ([Grollemund et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1503793112)).

The expansion was not a clean replacement. Bantu speakers took on substantial gene flow from the groups they met: rainforest hunter-gatherers in western Central Africa, Afroasiatic speakers in eastern Africa and San groups in southern Africa, and this admixture carried adaptive variation at the HLA and LCT loci ([Patin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal1988)). Admixture of this kind is the main reason the serial-founder diversity decline was not detected in earlier studies ([Fortes-Lima et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06770-6)). In KwaZulu-Natal, Iron Age individuals 300 to 500 years old are already genetically similar to present-day Bantu speakers, while 2,000-year-old hunter-gatherers from the same region resemble southern San groups ([Schlebusch et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao6266)).

The two models disagree chiefly on the first leg: straight south through the rainforest, or east and around it along savannah corridors. They converge on the same crossroads in Zambia and the DRC, and on the same endpoints.

## Central African hunter-gatherers

Central African hunter-gatherers (CAHG, also rainforest hunter-gatherers) are the deepest-branching lineage in the continent's forest belt, and the one most thoroughly reshaped by contact with farmers. They fall into a western group — Baka, Bakola, Bedzan, Biaka — in Cameroon, Gabon, the Republic of Congo and the Central African Republic, and an eastern group — Mbuti, Efe, Batwa — in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, Rwanda and Burundi. Structure and farmer ancestry vary considerably between these groups, which reflects the separate contact histories each has had with its agricultural neighbours ([Patin et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms4163)).

**Divergence depth.** Multilocus resequencing places the divergence of CAHG and farmer ancestors at roughly 60,000 years ago, with the western and eastern CAHG groups separating around 20,000 years ago — about when the rainforest contracted into refugia ([Patin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1000448)). Admixture-graph work using ancient DNA puts the CAHG lineage deeper than that, separating from other African lineages at close to the same time as southern African hunter-gatherers, or perhaps slightly earlier ([Lipson et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1929-1)). This is the result that displaced the older textbook ordering in which the Khoe-San split first and everyone else followed.

**The one ancient-DNA anchor.** The Shum Laka children in Cameroon, dated to roughly 8,000 and 3,000 years ago, have genome-wide profiles most similar to present-day western CAHG — in a region now occupied almost entirely by Bantu speakers ([Lipson et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1929-1)). There is no comparable ancient sample from the Congo basin itself.

**Admixture with farmers.** Present-day CAHG populations carry up to 50% farmer genomic ancestry, and the substantial part of that admixture began only within the last 1,000 years ([Patin et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms4163)) — long after agriculture reached the region. The flow ran both ways: Bantu-speaking populations in western Central Africa took on CAHG ancestry, and that admixture carried adaptive variation at the HLA locus ([Patin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal1988)). Any analysis treating a CAHG sample as an unadmixed forager reference will therefore be wrong by a large margin.

**Archaic ancestry.** Model-based analysis of whole-genome data supports archaic introgression in western CAHG ([Hsieh et al. 2016b](https://genome.cshlp.org/content/26/3/291)), and a separate analysis of deeply sequenced African genomes estimates about 4.3% archaic ancestry in Mbuti from an early-diverging, now-extinct modern human lineage ([Genome Biology 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-019-1684-5)) \[verify: author list\]. As in West Africa, these estimates are model-dependent and are contested by weakly structured stem models.

**The short-stature phenotype is convergent.** Admixture mapping in the Batwa of Uganda identified 16 genomic regions associated with the pygmy phenotype; the same regions show no elevated selection or differentiation signal in the Baka of Cameroon and Gabon, which implies the phenotype arose independently in eastern and western groups rather than once in a shared ancestor ([Perry et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1402875111)).

**Language and culture run on a different clock.** CAHG groups today speak languages acquired from neighbouring farmers. Combining musical instruments, foraging tools, specialised vocabulary and genome-wide data from 10 CAHG populations shows large-scale cultural interconnectivity among them both before and after the Bantu expansion, which the inherited languages conceal ([Padilla-Iglesias et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01891-y)).

**Sampling.** The genomic base is narrow. The whole-genome demographic and archaic-introgression analyses above rest on four Biaka and three Baka genomes ([Hsieh et al. 2016a](https://genome.cshlp.org/content/26/3/279)). For the lineage that may have split first from everyone else, that is a handful of individuals from two of perhaps a dozen populations, and no national genome programme covers the Congo basin.

## Documented admixture events

Almost no African population is a clean sample of a single ancestry, which is why admixture is now the organising frame for African population genetics rather than a correction applied afterwards ([Pfennig et al. 2023](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/15/4/evad054/7092825)).

| Event | Region | What the genetics show | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deep archaic (ghost) introgression | West Africa | 2–19% of ancestry from a lineage diverging before the Neanderthal split; contested by stem models | [Durvasula & Sankararaman 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aax5097) |
| Bantu expansion | Western Africa into subequatorial Africa, from 6,000–4,000 years ago | Diversity declines with distance from western Africa; Zambia and the DRC act as crossroads between routes | [Fortes-Lima et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06770-6) |
| Bantu speakers × local groups | Western Central, eastern and southern Africa | Gene flow from resident groups, carrying adaptive variation at HLA and LCT | [Patin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal1988) |
| West Eurasian back-migration | Horn of Africa and eastern Africa, around 3,000 years ago | Source closely related to Early Neolithic farmers; the 4,500-year-old Mota genome carries none of it | [Gallego Llorente et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aad2879) |
| East African / Eurasian gene flow into Khoe-San | Southern Africa | Every present-day Khoe-San group carries an estimated 9–30% | [Schlebusch et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao6266) |
| Austronesian–Bantu admixture | Madagascar, within the last millennium | All Malagasy descend from both sources; admixture sex-biased and geographically uneven, implying separate colonisations from Africa and Asia | [Pierron et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1704906114) |

One caution on the back-migration row. The original report claimed the West Eurasian signal reached Central, West and southern Africa, with 6–7% Eurasian ancestry in groups such as Yoruba and Mbuti. A processing error dropped homozygous-to-reference sites in the ancient genome; the published erratum withdraws the continent-wide extent and confines the detectable signal to eastern Africa ([Gallego Llorente et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf3945)). The Mota genome itself and the eastern African backflow stand.

The practical consequence for analysis: ancestry proportions estimated against present-day reference panels are generally estimating layered events, not one. Khoe-San reference samples carry eastern African and Eurasian ancestry; eastern African samples carry West Eurasian ancestry; most subequatorial samples carry both Bantu and local forager ancestry.

## Mitochondrial versus nuclear relationships

They do differ, in two distinct ways that are worth keeping apart: a statistical difference, because mtDNA is one non-recombining locus and therefore one realisation of the coalescent, and a biological difference, because gene flow in Africa has been strongly sex-biased and the maternal and paternal records of it point in opposite directions.

**Where they agree.** The deepest branch of the maternal tree, L0, is concentrated in southern Africa, with L0d and L0k at high frequency in Khoe-San groups. That matches the genome-wide result that the Khoe-San carry the deepest divergence among living populations. On the top-level topology, the two systems tell the same story.

**Where the dates diverge.** The mtDNA coalescent is shallower than the nuclear one, and systematically so. Control-region and mitogenome work puts the coalescence of L0d at about 87 ± 18 ka and its divergence from the rest of L0 at about 96 ± 20 ka, which the authors note is shallow relative to whole-genome estimates — the same L0d2c split dated to roughly 29 ka on mtDNA against about 64 ka in a whole-genome study ([BMC Evolutionary Biology 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2148-13-56)). Set that against 120,000–135,000 years for the earliest population divergence under a structured-stem model ([Ragsdale et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06055-y)), or 350,000–260,000 years under a clean-split model ([Schlebusch et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao6266)). None of this is a contradiction: mitochondrial effective size is roughly a quarter of the autosomal one, so mtDNA coalesces sooner, and a single gene tree carries no internal replication, so its confidence intervals understate the real uncertainty about the population tree.

**Where they genuinely disagree: direction of gene flow.** Across Africa, paternal and maternal structure are not merely different in depth but different in shape. Y-chromosome variation correlates strongly with language and not at all with geography, with among-group variance of Φ꜀ᴛ = 0.21 by linguistic grouping against 0.06 by geography; mtDNA shows low among-group variance on both axes, 0.03 and 0.04 ([Eur J Hum Genet 2005](https://www.nature.com/articles/5201408)). Removing Bantu speakers destroys the Y–language correlation and strengthens the mtDNA–language one, which points directly at the Bantu expansion as the cause.

In southern Africa the asymmetry is explicit: Bantu-speaking agro-pastoralist populations carry an excess of autochthonous Khoe-San mtDNA lineages, while Khoe-San groups carry an excess of incoming Y lineages, with the intensity of the bias increasing from north to south ([Bajić et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.23694)). The reading is a rapid, male-dominated expansion in which incoming men took local wives. The same asymmetry appears in Central Africa, where Bantu farmers and rainforest hunter-gatherers share substantial deep maternal ancestry without comparable deep paternal ancestry ([Mol Biol Evol 2009](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/26/7/1581/1123707)). Madagascar shows the pattern again, with admixture between the Bantu and Austronesian sources sex-biased and geographically uneven ([Pierron et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1704906114)).

So a population's position in an mtDNA tree and its position in a genome-wide admixture analysis can be substantially different, and the gap is information rather than noise: the size of the discordance estimates the sex bias of the admixture that produced the population.

**One cautionary case.** Chan et al. assembled 1,217 L0 mitogenomes and inferred from them that anatomically modern humans emerged in the Makgadikgadi–Okavango palaeo-wetland around 200 ka ([Chan et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1714-1)). The inference drew sustained criticism for treating a single gene tree as a population history and for setting aside the fossil and genome-wide evidence for structured, pan-African origins ([J Archaeol Sci 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440321000443)). The general lesson holds beyond that case: mtDNA locates maternal lineages, not populations, and it is blind to much of what the autosomes record — the ghost-archaic ancestry inferred in West and Central Africa, for instance, has no mitochondrial signature at all.

## Status of genome sequencing across African populations

The continent holding the most variation supplies the least data. By the GWAS Diversity Barometer, 0.15% of genome-wide association study participants are from Africa ([Alimohamed et al. 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-74952-7)). A separate audit of the GWAS Diversity Monitor as of September 2024 put European-labelled participants at 94.48% and Asian at 3.96%, with every other group below 1% ([Cell Genomics 2024](<https://www.cell.com/cell-genomics/fulltext/S2666-979X(24)00353-7>)) \[verify: first author\]. The two figures differ because they count continental origin and ancestry label differently; both leave the same conclusion.

The structural picture as of mid-2026: only a few African countries have formally launched government-driven national genome projects — Tunisia, Egypt and Senegal — while Tanzania, South Africa and Kenya have announced them, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ghana, Uganda, Botswana and the Gambia are building national efforts on existing research data ([Alimohamed et al. 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-74952-7)). Federated access, in which analysis runs inside the country of origin, is the model gaining ground for reconciling data sharing with national data sovereignty.

&#91;embedded content: national genome projects in Africa · status as of June 2026\]

The launched projects sit at the continental margins — two in North Africa, one in the far west — while the regions carrying the deepest genetic structure, the Congo basin and the Kalahari, have no national programme behind them.

Where the gaps still are, in sampling terms: hunter-gatherer and other small, deeply diverging groups are represented by tens of genomes rather than thousands; the Sahel and much of Central Africa remain thin; and a disproportionate share of "African" GWAS participants are African Americans, whose genomes carry a narrow slice of continental ancestry shaped by the transatlantic slave trade and subsequent admixture ([Patin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal1988)).

## What you can download, and what you must apply for

The split is close to a rule: global reference panels are open, and almost everything generated on the African continent in the last decade sits behind a data access committee. That is deliberate — controlled access is how African consortia reconcile broad consent with data sovereignty, not an administrative accident.

What an application involves, for the controlled sets: registration with the EGA, a research-use statement that the committee checks against the study's consent terms, confirmation that you are a bona fide researcher, and an institutional signatory on a data access agreement. Access is granted for a fixed period and must be renewed, and a new research aim needs a new request rather than an amendment of scope ([H3Africa Data Access Agreement](https://h3africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/App-F-H3Africa-Data-Access-Agreement-2020-.pdf)). The H3Africa policy additionally gives priority to researchers on the African continent or working with African collaborators, and prohibits resale of data elements.

Two practical points. First, summary statistics and allele-frequency panels derived from controlled datasets are often released openly even when individual-level data are not, so check whether frequencies alone would answer the question before applying. Second, open does not mean unexamined: a 2025 review of HGDP data use found broad re-use possibly inconsistent with the original consent understandings ([Nature Genetics 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-025-02412-x)) \[verify: author list\], which is part of why newer African datasets are governed the way they are.
