def abline(slope, intercept, ax=None):
    "Add a straight line through the plot"
    if ax is None:
        ax = plt.gca()
    x_vals = np.array(ax.get_xlim())
    y_vals = intercept + slope * x_vals
    ax.plot(x_vals, y_vals, '--', color='grey')
    
def add_lowess(x, y, ax=None, color=None, is_sorted=True, frac=0.005, it=0, **kwargs):
    "Add a lowess curve to the plot"
    if ax is None:
        ax = plt.gca() 
    filtered = lowess(y, x, is_sorted=is_sorted, frac=frac, it=it, **kwargs)
    ax.plot(filtered[:,0], filtered[:,1])

def stairs(df, start='start', end='end', pos='pos', endtrim=0):
    "Turn a df with start, end into one with pos to plot as stairs"
    df1 = df.copy(deep=True)

    df1[pos] = df1[start]
    df2[pos] = df2[end] - endtrim
    return pd.concat([df1, df2]).sort_values([start, end])

def pca(matrix, labels=None, n_components=2):
    sklearn_pca = PCA(n_components=n_components)
    sklearn_pca.fit(matrix)
    transform = sklearn_pca.transform(matrix)
    df = pd.DataFrame()

    if labels is not None:
        df['labels'] = labels

There are many papers and reviews that describe the dynamic of the FMR1 alleles across generations and how instability in the grey/intermediate zone leads to full expansion in the female germline. An important aspect that has come out in the last decade is that the intermediate alleles are also associated with a phenotype called FAXTAS (Fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome) and in females is also associated with premature ovarian insufficiency (FXPOI). Because the intermediate alleles in oocytes are deleterious (premature ovarian failure), the full mutations are selected for in the female germline – which explained the maternal transmission of full alleles and genetic anticipation.

On the male side, things are different, and variation occurs in the normal to grey/intermediate zone only (PMCID: http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/pmc1914886/ ) – but interestingly, grey/intermediate fathers never transmit full mutations to their daughters – moreover,  in the sperm of Frax male with full mutations (in blood), the allele is always retracted in sperm and testes – suggesting that there is a negative selection on full mutations in the male germline (doi: 10.1038/ng0693-143).

# Evolutionary strata on X

As we discussed, it is pretty amazing that the even X/Y transmission in spermatogenesis works at all if all an X (or Y) gene, essential to late spermatids/spermatozoa, needs to do is delay its expression until it can no longer be shared with cells with the Y (or X), instead of expressing early and handing it to the Y (or X) it through the chromatoid body. It must have taken a lot of selection to ensure this did not happen, as additional X and Y chromosome chunks stopped recombining. Maybe by moving offending genes to the autosomes, by adding compensating gametologs to the other sex chromosome (like VCY copied from VCX in the human-chimp ancestor), or by moving control of X and Y genes the autosomes (like PRDM9 seems to affect VCX transcription). On the other hand, the sex chromosomes will have opposed these processes, E.g., by duplicating genes (like SLY/SLX in mice). As you said, it may be possible to see these processes play out by looking at the expression of genes lost and duplicated and whether the time of loss/gain of genes relevant to spermatogenesis correlates with the time of the different strata on X.
